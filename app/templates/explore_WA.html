Regional Heritage

Explore WA: Our Place in AI History

Western Australia's story within global AI development is unique — shaped by its vast geography, dominant industries, world-class universities, and growing technology sector.

🗺️
Why Western Australia Matters

Western Australia occupies a third of the Australian continent, with a population concentrated in Perth and a vast regional hinterland dependent on mining, agriculture, and environmental management. These conditions have driven unique applications of AI — from autonomous mining vehicles in the Pilbara to precision agriculture in the Wheatbelt, from marine conservation AI on the Ningaloo coast to remote healthcare delivery across distances greater than most European countries.

WA's universities — UWA, Curtin, Murdoch, and ECU — have contributed to AI research across every paradigm represented in this museum. The state's technology sector, centred in Perth, is rapidly growing and positioning itself as a hub for AI innovation in the Indo-Pacific region.

🎓

Universities

UWA, Curtin, ECU, and Murdoch have strong computer science and AI research programs, with alumni contributing to global AI companies and research institutions.

🏢

Tech Sector

Perth's tech sector is growing rapidly, with startups and established companies applying AI to mining, healthcare, agriculture, and environmental management.

⛏️

Resources Industry

WA's mining giants (Rio Tinto, BHP, Fortescue) operate some of the world's most advanced autonomous and AI-driven operations, pioneered right here.

WA Research and Computing Heritage

Western Australia’s AI history is reflected not only in one institution, but across a wider network of universities, research infrastructure, and industry practice. UWA, Curtin, ECU, and Murdoch have all contributed to AI teaching and research, while facilities such as the Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre have supported large-scale data processing, machine learning, and scientific computing.

Across the museum’s ten topics, the WA context shows a consistent pattern: global AI ideas were often adapted locally through teaching, research, infrastructure, mining, transport, agriculture, and decision-support systems. This gives the museum a regional perspective that connects international AI history with Western Australian experience.

WA Context Across the 10 AI Topics

This section summarises the Western Australian context researched across all 10 AI topics in the museum. It highlights how local teaching, research, infrastructure, public guidance, and industry practice connect WA to major developments in artificial intelligence.

1. Alan Turing & The Turing Test

In WA, Turing’s behaviour-based view of intelligence can be seen in chatbots, automated support systems, and digital services that are evaluated by how effectively they respond to users. The topic also connects indirectly to WA through Professor Jeff Rohl at UWA, whose academic background links WA computing culture to ideas still shaped by Turing’s influence.

2. Learning Machines

In Western Australia, the influence of learning machines appears in UWA teaching and research in data science, in the Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre’s support for large-scale data processing and model training, and in Rio Tinto’s use of AI and data science for predictive maintenance and operational improvement.

3. Board Game Playing

In WA, board game AI is especially significant in education. UWA teaching materials use minimax, alpha-beta pruning, and adversarial search to introduce core ideas in decision-making and optimisation, showing how game environments remain useful AI training grounds in the state.[1]

4. Expert Systems

In Western Australia, expert-system and decision-support approaches were relevant in large industrial environments such as Alcoa’s Wagerup alumina refinery, where expert knowledge and rule-based support could assist with diagnostics, scheduling, and operational planning.[2]

5. Artificial Neural Networks

WA’s role in neural networks is reflected through university research at UWA, Curtin, ECU, and Murdoch, support from CSIRO and the Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre, and data-intensive projects such as the Square Kilometre Array, where large-scale computation and analysis are essential.[3][4]

6. Internet-Driven AI Systems

Internet-driven AI is especially important in WA’s mining and energy sectors. Rio Tinto’s “Mine of the Future” and BHP’s intelligent data platforms show how internet-connected systems, remote operations in Perth, and large-scale data analysis support decision-making across geographically distant industrial sites.[5][6]

7. Evolutionary Computing

In WA, evolutionary computing has been applied to infrastructure optimisation. UWA-linked research used problem-specific genetic algorithms for sparse power distribution planning in the South-West and for multi-objective optimisation in road widening and maintenance scheduling.[7][8]

8. Synthetic Media Technology

WA’s connection to synthetic media lies in local strengths in computer vision, machine learning, and pattern recognition, together with the influence of Australia’s eSafety response to deepfake risks. In practice, this means WA engages with synthetic media through research capability, digital literacy, and harm-prevention frameworks.[9]

9. Natural Language Processing

In Western Australia, NLP research is supported by UWA, Curtin, ECU, Murdoch, and CSIRO. Facilities such as Pawsey provide the computing power needed for large-scale NLP work, while projects such as the SKA create demand for information extraction, metadata analysis, and automated processing of large volumes of text and scientific data.[4][10]

10. Large Language Models

WA’s LLM capability is reflected in academic research at UWA, applied autonomous-system projects at Curtin, and practical uptake in public-sector governance through WA guidance on large language models. This shows WA’s role in adaptation, deployment, and responsible use rather than in building frontier foundation models.[11][12][13]

Taken together, these examples show that Western Australia’s place in AI history is not defined by a single invention, but by the ongoing local adaptation, teaching, application, and extension of major AI developments across research, infrastructure, industry, and public institutions.
